The governing rule

A program is tested against the standard its own published materials name. Never against whichever standard is convenient, and never against a default.

When the standard or the threshold is unknown, apply no income test. A missing limit must never quietly become a wrong one.

The failure this guards against is not a crash. It is a plausible-looking number that turns away a household the program was built to serve — and several of the standards below exist because two tables agreed to the dollar right up until they didn't.

Decision procedure

Work down the list and stop at the first match. The order is load-bearing. It mirrors income_standard_for() in scripts/load_data.py, so a person and the loader reach the same answer.

Why the agency's own table comes first

Section 8 eligibility is determined by the public housing authority that administers the voucher, and each PHA publishes limits for its own jurisdiction. Those derive from HUD's — HAJC's matches HUD-MFI at 50% to the dollar today — which is exactly why leaving a voucher on HUD's table is easy to miss: it gives the right number until the two diverge. They can: the effective dates already differ (HAJC 1 May 2026, HUD 1 June), and JHCDC's posted table is dated April 2025.

Cannot be inferred

The income text says “50% AMI”, which is equally true of HUD's table and the PHA's. So the authority is recorded per program in PROGRAM_STANDARD_OVERRIDES. A voucher program with no entry falls through to HUD-MFI and will quietly stop being right the moment its authority diverges.

Why SMI is checked before FPG

Minnesota's Energy Assistance Program tests at 50% SMI, but its write-up also mentions “sizes 19–20 use 110% FPG”. Reading that as the primary standard drops the four-person limit from $71,998 to $33,000.

This was wrong once

FPG was checked first in an earlier revision and every EAP program was reclassified as poverty-tested. Programs that genuinely test on poverty level never mention SMI at all, so SMI wins when both appear.

Why category is not used

ACCESS-RVAR-OAR-OR-001 sits under Down Payment Assistance but tests against “the State of Oregon Median Income Limit”. Classifying by category put it on county AMI and resolved its limit to $88,313 instead of the correct $103,342. The program's own text decides; category is a fallback only for rows with no income language.

The standards

HAJC-HCV · JHCDC-HCV

Housing authority voucher limits · 8 rows each
Used for
Section 8 Housing Choice Vouchers, NED vouchers, and the Homeownership Voucher — each under the authority that administers it. Three HAJC programs, one JHCDC.
Why this one
The PHA makes the eligibility decision and publishes the table an applicant is actually measured against.
Sources
HAJC: hajc.net (posted 1 May 2026)
JHCDC: jhcdc.net/applicants (posted 1 April 2025)
Published
By each authority after HUD issues new limits, on its own timing. That independence is the whole reason these are tracked separately.
Watch for

JHCDC publishes monthly figures, stored here annualised (× 12). Its posted table predates HUD's FY2026 limits and runs about 1.4% above them — most likely the page is stale rather than Josephine genuinely using higher thresholds. Confirm with JHCDC before relying on it for a determination.

HAJC's page also carries a stale 2021 table under its FAQ. The current figures are the ones under “Income limits”.

HUD-MFI

HUD Section 8 income limits · 904 rows · 28 counties
Used for
Rent assistance, most down-payment assistance — anything citing “area median income” that is not run by a PHA with its own table.
Source
huduser.gov/portal/datasets/il.html, via HUD's API (free token in HUD_API_TOKEN).
Published
Annually, spring → effective ~1 June.
Refresh
The only automated one. Run the Fetch HUD income limits workflow with the new year.
Watch for

The 30% tier is not 30% of median. HUD's “extremely low income” figure is the greater of 30% AMI or the poverty guideline, so for larger households the poverty floor takes over — Jackson's four-person ELI is $33,000 where a straight 30% would be $29,450. Tiers are not proportional; scaling between them is an estimate and must never exclude anyone.

HUD-MTSP

Multifamily Tax Subsidy Project limits · 400 income, 420 rent · 2024–2026
Used for
LIHTC at whichever tier the project elected (20–80%), Tax-Exempt Bonds, LIFT rental at 60%, LIFT homeownership at 80%.
Why this one
None of those publishes its own figures. All test against MTSP, so one table answers all three.
Source
OHCS workbook (Open Data Download on the OHCS dashboard), “Actual Income & Rent Limits” tab.
Published
Annually ~1 May; owners implement by mid-June.

Why not HUD-MFI: they agree at 50% and 60% for Jackson, then diverge at 80% — $78,480 against $78,500 for four people. HUD caps each Section 8 tier separately; every MTSP tier is an exact multiple of the 50% figure. Small here, far wider in HERA-special areas.

HUD-MTSP-HERA

MTSP HERA Special · 400 income, 350 rent · routed to nothing
Used for
Nothing, by default — and that is deliberate.
What it is
Housing and Economic Recovery Act limits, applying only to LIHTC projects placed in service before 2009. Newer projects use HUD-MTSP. For Jackson County it runs $2,350–$3,760 above Actual at 50–80%.
Source
OHCS workbook, “MTSP HERA” tab; huduser.gov MTSP.
Not a county-level dataset

Which table applies is a property of the building, not the county, and the program matrix does not carry placed-in-service dates. Routing a program here would raise its limits for every building it does not apply to. It exists so a caseworker looking at a specific older property can resolve the right figure.

HUD-HOME · HUD-HTF

HOME/CDBG and Housing Trust Fund · 192 + 48 income, 126 + 42 rent
Used for
HOME Investment Partnerships and CDBG at 30/50/60/80%; the national Housing Trust Fund at a single ELI tier. No program in the matrix uses either yet.
Why separate from HUD-MFI
HOME limits match Section 8 at 50% and 80% but differ at 30%, where HOME publishes the unadjusted figure and Section 8 applies the poverty floor. Different programs, different tables.
Sources
HOME · HTF
Published
Annually, effective ~1 June.

HOME/CDBG also publishes three kinds of rent for the same unit — Fair Market Rent, High Rent Limit, Low Rent Limit — which is why rent limits have their own table.

OHCS-MIRL · OHCS-THGF

Moderate Income Rural Limit · Tribal Housing Grant Fund · 192 + 64 income
Used for
Oregon's Moderate-Income Revolving Loan program and Tribal Housing Grant Fund, at 50/80/100/120%. No program uses either yet.
Why two standards for identical figures
They are separately administered, published on separate tabs, with separate effective dates (MIRL 1 June, THGF 30 June). They agree today; keeping them apart means a future divergence shows up as data rather than as a wrong answer.
Source
huduser.gov CDBG limits, via the OHCS workbook.

Both are proportional — the 100% row is OHCS's median and every tier is an exact multiple of it.

OR-SMI · MN-SMI

State median income · 24 + 48 rows · statewide
Used for
Oregon utility and energy programs at 60% SMI; Minnesota's Energy Assistance Program and the programs keyed to it at 50% SMI.
Why this one
These test against state median, not the county's. For a two-person household, 60% of Oregon SMI is $50,194 while Josephine's 60% AMI is $40,140.
Sources
Oregon · Minnesota — HHS via the LIHEAP Clearinghouse.
Published
Each federal fiscal year, summer → effective 1 October.
The trap

For a four-person household, Oregon at 60% is $73,816 and Minnesota at 50% is $73,969 — 0.2% apart. A program on the wrong state's table looks correct in every spot check and is still wrong. The tier differs by state too; copying it across is as wrong as copying the table.

HHS-FPG

Federal poverty guidelines · 36 rows · national
Used for
Emergency General Assistance (Stearns, Benton, Sherburne) and the four FHPAP providers — all at 200% FPG.
Source
aspe.hhs.gov.
Published
Each January, in the Federal Register.

Only the 100% figures are stored; multiples like 200% are exact arithmetic. The 2026 guidelines for households of 3–8 match HUD's Jackson County ELI figures to the dollar — two independent sources agreeing.

USDA-502-GUARANTEED

USDA Section 502 rural home loans · 168 rows · 28 counties
Used for
Section 502 guaranteed loans at 115% (moderate income); the very-low and low rows are published alongside.
Source
HB-1-3555 Appendix 5, issued as a procedure notice each federal fiscal year (FY2026: PN 657, 13 July 2026); rd.usda.gov handbooks.
Status
USDA-502-DIRECT is declared but empty — the Direct appendix (HB-1-3550) has not been supplied.
Why not borrow HUD's table

For 22 of 24 Oregon area/tier combinations the two agree to the dollar. Grants Pass is the exception, running about 1.4% higher at every tier and bracket. A USDA program measured against HUD would be right in eleven counties and wrong in Josephine — the hardest kind of error to notice.

USDA publishes in brackets — one figure for 1–4 persons, another for 5–8 — stored as published and expanded on lookup. And its “115%” is defined as the greater of three formulas, so the standard is not marked proportional.

Corrected

An earlier load carried two Jackson rows from secondary sources ($119,850 against the published $122,800). They were removed in migration 0010 rather than kept as history, because they were never accurate for any period.

OHCS-BOND

OHCS Flex Lending & Mortgage Credit Certificate · no rows
Status
Declared but empty. Both programs currently carry no income test. OHCS publishes these limits by county, household size, and targeted-area status, but no stable table has been found. A known gap, not an oversight.

Rent limits

Rent limits live in their own table, rent_limits, because they are a different shape: they vary by bedroom count rather than household size, and HOME/CDBG publishes three kinds of rent for the same unit, which income_limits has no column for. 

select rent_limit_for('HUD-MTSP', 'OR-JACKSON', 2, 60);
    -- 1324   (2-bedroom, 60% tier, monthly)
    
    select rent_limit_for('HUD-HOME', 'OR-JACKSON', 2, null, 'high_rent');
    -- 1413

Arguments: (standard, area, bedrooms, tier, kind, as_of_date). Tier is NULL for kinds that are not tier-based. Newest effective date wins, as with income — a 2024 as-of query returns that year's $1,186.

Nothing in the screener reads rents yet. They are loaded so the data is in place for program-side work — the maximum rent a voucher covers, whether a unit falls under a project's cap — rather than for applicant screening. 1,130 rows across six standards.

Adding or updating a limit table

  • Get the figures from the publisher named above. Never from a summary, a search result, or a secondary site — those have been wrong three times in this project.
  • Put them in the loader's CSV format, documented in scripts/load_income_limits.py (or load_rent_limits.py).
  • Run the Load income limits workflow; fill rent_csv_path to load rents in the same run.

The loader upserts rather than truncating, so limits accumulate and past determinations stay reproducible. An unknown standard_id or area_id aborts the load.

Superseding is not replacing

The upsert key includes effective_date. Loading a new year adds rows alongside the old — correct when the old rows were genuinely in force, wrong when they were mistaken. Replacing provisional figures with published ones needs a deliberate delete, as in migration 0010.

Validate before trusting

  • Tier ordering. 30% < 50% < 60% < 80% at every household size; 5–8 bracket above 1–4.
  • Known relationships. HUD's 60% row is exactly 1.2× its 50% row; MTSP tiers are exact multiples of the 50% row.
  • Match against the source itself, not a neighbouring dataset. The Minnesota USDA figures were confirmed by locating every value in the source text, which is the check that catches a parsing error. Comparing to HUD would not have — each program uses a specific dataset, and one does not override the other.
Why the checks exist

A parsing bug silently filled every 80% row with 50% figures, because LOW INCOME also matches inside VERY LOW INCOME. Spot checks passed; tier ordering exposed it. Another flattened nested arrays so both USDA brackets took the same value; ordering caught that too.

What the screener does with all this

  • The standard and tier live in program_income_rules, derived from each program's own text at load time. They are data, not logic.
  • A missing tier means no income test, not a default.
  • A missing limit returns NULL, and the program stays in results with a “worth checking” note.
  • Being over a limit on gross income flags rather than excludes — a typed figure is an estimate, and programs measure income their own way (some count only the most recent month). Only incomes more than 10% over the limit are filtered.
  • Household sizes 1–8 are published; 9–12 extrapolate by HUD's rule — add 8% of the 4-person limit per extra person. Exact for proportional standards, an estimate for the rest, and an estimate never excludes anyone.
  • Scaling to an unstored tier is exact for HHS-FPG HUD-MTSP HUD-MTSP-HERA OR-SMI MN-SMI OHCS-MIRL OHCS-THGF, and an estimate for the rest.
